# Writing a PhD Thesis in Engineering — UB Guide Steps

Distilled from `report/The-UB-Thesis-and-Dissertation-Guide-2.pdf`
(*The University of Buea Thesis and Dissertation Guide*, UB Postgraduate School, 2021, 124 pp).

Section references below are to the guide's own numbering, with guide page numbers where useful.

## 0. Which chapter template applies to engineering

The mapping table in §2.22 (guide p.71) routes each domain to a proposed table of contents:

| Domain | Template |
|---|---|
| Fundamental Sciences | Appendix III A |
| **Applied Sciences and disciplines at the interface** | **Appendix III B** |
| Arts (Literatures) | Appendix III C (C1–C5) |
| Education and some Social Sciences | Appendix III D |
| Geography and Social Sciences | Appendix III E |

Engineering falls under **Applied Sciences and disciplines at the interface**.

> ⚠️ **Known inconsistency in the guide.** The table above points applied sciences to Appendix III B,
> but the appendix headings themselves are swapped: the page headed
> *"PROPOSED TABLE OF CONTENT … IN THE APPLIED SCIENCES"* is **Appendix III A** (guide p.96),
> while **Appendix III B** (guide p.97) is headed *"… IN THE FUNDAMENTAL SCIENCES"*.
> The two skeletons are near-identical. Use the applied-sciences skeleton (reproduced below)
> and confirm the choice with your Departmental Scientific Committee.

### Chapter skeleton (applied sciences)

```
Preliminary pages
  Title page
  Blank page
  Repeat of title page
  Dedication            (Roman numeral ii)
  Certification         (iii)
  Acknowledgements      (iv)
  Abstract              (v)
  Table of contents     (vi)
  List of illustrations · abbreviations · figures · tables · appendices

Chapter One: Introduction
  1.1 Background to the study
  1.2 Problem statement
  1.3 Rationale of the study
  1.4 Objectives and/or hypothesis

Chapter Two: Review of the Literature
  2.1 Historical overview of the theory and research literature
  2.2 The theory and research literature specific to the topic
  2.3 Research in cognate areas relevant to the topic
  2.4 Critique of current literature and summary of known results
  2.5 The contribution this study will make to the literature

Chapter Three: Materials and Methods
  3.1 The methodology
  3.2 Specific procedures
  3.3 Research population or sample
  3.4 Instrumentation (pilot study, data collection)
  3.5 Treatment and analysis of the data
  3.6 Ethical considerations
  3.7 Scope of the current research

Chapter Four: Results and Applications
  4.1 Results and outputs
  4.2 Applications

Chapter Five: Discussion, Conclusions, Recommendations, Perspectives
  5.1 Discussion
  5.2 Conclusions
  5.3 Recommendations
  5.4 Perspectives for further research

Bibliography / References
Appendices
Author's index          (PhD only)
Subject index           (PhD only)
Curriculum vitae        (PhD only, ≤ 2 pages, after the bibliography)
```

Keep chapter lengths balanced. The guide's own example: a 100-page, five-chapter
document should run roughly 20 pages per chapter (§2.22, guide p.72).

## 1. Admission, coursework, comprehensive exam

- Validate **at least 60 credits** of coursework, including the comprehensive examination (§5.4, item 4).
- Duration of the PhD: **6 to 10 semesters** — 3 years, extendable to 5
  (§2.5; Art. 15 of Order No. 99/003/MINESUP/DDES).
- Entry floor: no admission into the doctorate cycle without a master's mark of **at least 12/20**
  (Order 18/00167, Section 9.3).

## 2. Supervisor and topic registration

- The choice of supervisor is at the student's discretion, within university regulations
  (Order 18/00167, Section 6.1). The supervisor must be of magisterial rank specialising in your
  research domain (Section 4.1). Where the work is professional in nature, the supervisor is
  assisted by a high-level professional (Section 4.3).
- Supervision load caps: no more than **10 theses and 10 dissertations** per teacher,
  extendable by derogation to 15 (Section 11).
- After supervisor approval, submit the topic to the Head of Department, who transmits it
  upward. The topic must be recorded at the **Central Thesis and Dissertations Registry within
  one month** of submission (Art. 14).

## 3. Timeline / action plan

Draw up the 36-step timeline form (**Appendix V**, guide pp.110–111), endorsed by your
supervisor(s) and the Head of Establishment, and lodged with the Directorate of Postgraduate
Studies for follow-up (§2.4, guide p.46). It runs from registration through to
"Final approval and deposition of thesis to the Library".

## 4. Research proposal

Minimal contents (**Appendix I**, guide p.93):

```
I.   Introduction
II.  The Problem
     a. Rationale, significance, or need for the study
     b. Theoretical framework for the proposed study
     c. Statement of the problem
     d. Elements, hypotheses, theories, or research questions to be investigated
     e. Delimitations and limitations of the study
     f. Definition of terms
     g. Summary
III. Review of the Literature
     a. Historical overview of the theory and research literature
     b. Theory and research literature specific to the topic
     c. Research in cognate areas
     d. Critique of the validity of appropriate theory and research literature
     e. Summary of what is known and unknown about the proposed topic
     f. The contribution this study will make to the literature
IV.  Research Procedures
     a. Research methodology       e. Pilot study
     b. Specific procedures        f. Data collection
     c. Research population/sample g. Treatment of the data
     d. Instrumentation            h. Summary
V.   Appendices
VI.  Bibliography
```

§2.17 (guide p.62) lists 11 questions a well-conceived proposal must answer before it reaches
your supervisor — title, purpose, sequential steps, help needed, resources, people involved,
what a typical working day looks like, how you will demonstrate success, costs and funding,
time frame per activity, and whether you have consulted the UB proposal format.

The proposal will be scored against the 29-item evaluation form in **Appendix II**
(guide pp.94–95), completed by each member of the Departmental Scientific Committee.

## 5. Seminar I — proposal defence (35%)

Public research seminar before the Departmental Scientific Committee (§2.18, guide pp.64–65).
Graded. Three possible outcomes, always notified in writing:

1. **Rejection** — rare; a new proposal is required.
2. **Approval with conditions** — by far the most frequent.
3. **Unconditional approval**.

Acceptance of the proposal *is* the formal acceptance of the thesis topic by the Committee.

## 6. Conduct the research + intermediate seminars

- **Seminar II — progress report seminar (25%)**, held midway through the research.
- PhD candidates, unlike master's candidates, are expected to deliver **additional intermediate
  seminars between Seminars I and III** (§2.18, guide p.65), and to participate in the doctoral
  programme's research seminars, presenting major advances at each critical step (Art. 11.2).
- Grading teams must include an academic from outside your programme or department.

## 7. Publish — mandatory before defence

- At least **one peer-reviewed publication** from the thesis is required, and the requirement
  **cannot be relaxed** for a PhD (§2.4, guide p.45) — unlike the master's, where it can be.
- The expected count for pure and applied sciences is 4, **but** the guide explicitly notes that in
  **Mathematics, Physics and Engineering Sciences** it may not be possible to fractionate a thesis
  into several publications; in that case the Department determines whether the thesis can be
  defended on its contents.
- The publication must appear in the signed CV submitted with your defence file (§5.4, item 8).
- Start this around Seminar II. It is the gate that most often delays engineering candidates.

## 8. Write-up strategy

Two sanctioned approaches (§2.22, guide pp.70–71):

| Approach | How it works | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **Piecemeal** | Write and publish each chunk as it is finished | Preferred where the topic fractionates (usually true in engineering); secures priority through regular publication. Risk: no bird's-eye view; early chapters can go stale |
| **Integrated** | Write intro + literature review while finalising analysis; results, discussion and conclusions last | Coherent whole, but details fade with time — demands rigorous note-keeping, or you end up repeating experiments |

Whichever you pick: do not leave everything to the tail end, and the supervisor is not expected
to write the thesis for you or to override your style by over-editing.

## 9. Format rules

The PhD format equals the master's format (§3.1–3.8) with the PhD deltas in §4.1.

| Item | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Length | **150–250 pages** (Pure and Applied Sciences); upper limit exceedable by 10% |
| Abstract | **≤ 400 words** (300 for master's) — problem, method, principal arguments, findings, conclusions/recommendations |
| Degree wording | "Doctor of Philosophy Degree" in title, certification and all relevant sections |
| Indices | **Author index and subject index** at the end (PhD only) |
| CV | **≤ 2 pages**, inserted after the bibliography; academic and employment record, publications, marking those derived from the thesis |
| Paper | Good-quality white A4, printed on one side only |
| Spacing | Double-spaced throughout, except quotations and footnotes (single); one extra line between paragraphs |
| Margins | **3.5 cm left**, **2.5 cm** on the other three edges |
| Page numbers | Upper right-hand corner, at least **1.5 cm** into the page |
| Front matter numbering | Roman numerals: dedication ii, certification iii, acknowledgements iv, abstract v, contents vi |
| Page order | (1) title page, (2) **blank page**, (3) repeat of title page, (4) dedication, (5) certification |
| Acknowledgements | Must not exceed one page, and not be superfluous |
| Word breaks | Do not hyphenate/break words at line ends |
| Quotations | Keep brief; > 4 typed lines → new line, indented 1.5 cm, single-spaced, no quotation marks |
| Citations | Style accepted in your discipline; the style manual must be approved by the Departmental Scientific Committee (§2.18, item 5) |
| Certification page | Two versions — pre-defence and post-defence forms (Appendix VI, guide pp.114–115) |
| Binding for examination | Soft/loose temporary binding first; hard cover only for the final corrected version |

### Title page contents (§3.2.1)

1. The University of Buea
2. Faculty and Department in which the research was conducted
3. Full title (and subtitle, if any)
4. The qualification — e.g. *"A thesis submitted … in partial fulfilment of the requirements for the degree of Doctor of Philosophy in …"*
5. Full name of the author (matriculation number and previous qualifications)
6. Full name and title of supervisor(s)
7. Month and year of submission

## 10. Seminar III — pre-defence (40%)

You present the **complete first draft**. The Departmental Scientific Committee ascertains its
level of completeness and authorises the final set of corrections before binding and submission
(§2.18). Members score it using the thesis evaluation form; a synthesis goes into your file.

**Seminar weighting: I = 35%, II = 25%, III = 40%.** These are real marks, not formalities.

## 11. Defence file — 13 items (§5.4, guide pp.82–83)

Constituted by the candidate, submitted to the Postgraduate School through Department and Faculty:

1. Official admission letter showing date of first admission and any special conditions
2. Fee receipts + fees clearance certificate from Receipt Control
3. Signed **Form B** showing timely course registration
4. Official transcript: coursework completed + comprehensive examination passed (≥ 60 credits)
5. Proof of seminars attended and delivered, in the Department and in academic meetings
6. Document showing official designation of supervisor(s)
7. **Two loosely bound copies** of the thesis, endorsed by the supervisor(s) on the certification page
8. Signed and dated **CV** with publication list — at least one from this research
9. Signed and dated **pre-defence report** by the supervisor(s)
10. Minutes and recommendations of the **Departmental** Scientific Committee
11. Minutes of the **Faculty/School** Scientific Committee, plus a short report on how you addressed the issues they raised
12. Proposed names and addresses of **two internal (UB) and two external (non-UB) reviewers**
13. Proposed defence panel, endorsed by both Scientific Committees

*Items 1–8 come from you; item 9 from your supervisor; items 10–13 from Department and Faculty.*

The supervisor's PhD pre-defence report must cover, at minimum (§5.6): identity of the student;
statement of the problem; working and learning etiquette; results obtained; **originality of the
work**; exposure and additional skills acquired; level of completion; methodology used;
suitability for onward transmission; proposed reviewers and panellists.

## 12. Plagiarism check and external review (§5.5)

- The Faculty's research office runs the university-provided **plagiarism software** over the entire
  work. The document is validated only after the output is interpreted against accepted standards.
- The Academic Office then selects **one internal and one external reviewer**, asked whether the
  thesis meets the international standard for a doctoral thesis, whether the results are consistent
  with the stated objectives, and whether the scientific approach is sound.

| Reviewer outcome | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Both positive | Academic Office recommends the viva; VC authorises the oral examination |
| Contradictory | A third reviewer is sought; the majority verdict (2 of 3) is retained |
| Both negative | Thesis returned to the candidate to revise with the supervisor(s), typically ~3 months |

## 13. Viva voce (§4.3, §5.8)

- **Public**, unless the VC has granted an exemption for confidential content (Art. 18).
- Panel of **at least five (5)** senior academic staff approved by the Vice-Chancellor: the supervisor;
  three staff of Full/Associate Professor rank (or two plus one accredited to supervise research),
  **at least one from outside UB**; and a national or foreign personality from outside the University
  chosen for proven scientific or professional competence (Art. 17).
- **Total ≤ 3 hours.** You present for **40–45 minutes**; the panel then examines you for about two
  hours. The **reviewers question first**, then other panellists; the **supervisor(s) last**.
- Panel members wear academic robes. **The candidate is not robed.**
- Evaluation forms are collected twice: for the written document *before* the oral starts, and for
  the oral *after*. **Grading by consensus is formally prohibited** — individual marks are averaged.
  If the spread between highest and lowest exceeds 10 points, the Chairperson forces an adjustment.
- The outcome is announced immediately; exact marks are withheld pending Senate approval.

## 14. Corrections, binding, deposit (§5.9)

1. Make the corrections requested by the panel.
2. The **internal reviewer (normally Rapporteur II)** verifies the corrections and signs a clearance form.
3. Submit the **unbound (loose-leaf)** document in a folder to the Postgraduate School for format
   verification and approval.
4. On clearance, obtain the Head of Department's and Dean's signatures on the post-defence
   certification page.
5. Personally submit **at least three copies** to the librarian and pay binding fees. The library
   retains two copies after affixing the University logo and binding.
   *(Art. 21 additionally requires six bound copies to the Faculty/School after the defence.)*

You are considered to have completed the programme only **after the public defence and approval
by Senate** (§5.7).

## What the examiners are actually judging

- **Originality, individuality, rigour** (§2.6). Originality is satisfied by attacking an unresolved
  problem, or replicating known results by a substantially different approach with a reasonable
  prospect of advancing understanding. Individuality means the study is conceived, conducted and
  reported primarily by you — even if the topic was suggested by your supervisor.
- **The seven marks of scholarly work** (§2.8, after Mauch and Birch, 1988): published in a
  respected refereed venue; exposed long enough to withstand peer criticism; grounded in the
  expert literature, with a rational defence for any departure from it; evidence of a thorough,
  critical, analytic mind that tests its own hypotheses; demonstration of systematic command of
  the field rather than scattered insights; non-polemical; and useful, as evidenced by citation.
- Article 10 of Order 99/003 is the legal bar: the thesis must be **"a significant and original
  contribution to the advancement of knowledge"** in your discipline.
